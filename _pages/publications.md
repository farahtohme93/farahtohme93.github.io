## Working Papers

**[The Narrative Channel of Fiscal Policy](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6924640)**<br>
with Sarah Arndt<br>
<small>[SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6924640)</small>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Abstract</summary>

This paper investigates how media narratives on fiscal policy shape households' inflation expectations. We collect a large corpus of newspaper articles reporting on fiscal policy from four major German newspapers spanning 2006 to March 2025. Using a large language model (ChatGPT), we introduce a strategy to automatically identify different fiscal narratives in text and construct narrative indices from this data. We then estimate the effect of these narrative indices on household inflation expectations and find that three of the four indices have a positive and significant effect of varying size. Lastly, we measure how fiscal narratives affect the transmission of a government spending shock to the economy and find that some narratives amplify its impact while others dampen it.

</details>

**[Tariff Attention and Inflation Expectations: Evidence from Financial Markets](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6848605)**<br>
with Vandana Ramakrishnan<br>
<small>[SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6848605)</small>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Abstract</summary>

We study how tariff-attention shocks affect market-based inflation compensation in the United States. Using a Bloomberg tariff-news index to measure attention to trade policy, we estimate daily local projections of TIPS breakeven inflation rates and inflation swaps at maturities from one to thirty years over July 2018 to January 2026. The market response to tariff news reverses sign between the two Trump trade wars. In the full sample, a one-standard-deviation shock is followed by modest declines in both measures, concentrated at short maturities and consistent with a risk-off, growth-negative channel rather than pure cost-push inflation. This average masks sharp heterogeneity, however. In the first trade war (2018–2019), breakevens and swaps fall broadly across maturities. In the second (2025), inflation swaps rise significantly across the curve from the two- to the twenty-year maturity while breakevens decouple, falling at the short end but turning positive at longer horizons. Because swaps are not exposed to the TIPS liquidity premium, this short-end divergence between the two instruments isolates a flight-to-liquidity effect and identifies swaps as the cleaner gauge. These results suggest that markets may have assigned greater weight to persistent tariff-driven price pressures during the second episode.

</details>

**Fiscal Policy in the German Press: Twenty Years of Evidence∗**<br>
with Sarah Arndt<br>
<small>*Draft available upon request*</small>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Abstract</summary>

This paper examines how fiscal policy narratives evolved in the German press over the
past two decades. The analysis is based on a corpus of 514,940 newspaper articles covering
fiscal policy from four major German newspapers between January 2006 and March 2025.
Using a large language model, I classify the articles into four narrative categories: fiscal
expansion, public debt, government spending, and fiscal consolidation. The paper reports
five main findings. First, coverage of fiscal policy in the German press is dominated by
advocacy rather than explanation. Narratives prescribing what governments should do account
for 85% of all narrative articles, while the two narratives that primarily assess the
fiscal situation are never the most prevalent in any month. Second, I identify 104 narrative
episodes with a median duration of two months. Although the onset of these episodes is
not systematically associated with major fiscal events, the longest-lasting episodes typically
follow such events. Third, fiscal narratives are generally complementary rather than
competing. Every pair of narratives that can co-occur within an article appears together
more frequently than would be expected by chance. In addition, the prevalence of all four
narratives exhibits positive comovement over time, with narratives promoting fiscal policy
actions typically preceding those describing the consequences of such policies. Fourth, each
newspaper displays a distinct and persistent editorial stance on fiscal policy. The conservative
broadsheet presents the broadest range of fiscal arguments, the left-leaning newspaper
the narrowest, and the newspaper with by far the largest readership is the least representative
of the overall press. Finally, ideological differences across newspapers vary with the
political composition of the government. The distance between the conservative and leftleaning
newspapers is approximately three and a half times larger under the only left-led
government in the sample than under the three CDU/CSU-led governments. During the
left-led government, the left-leaning newspaper becomes more supportive of government
spending, while the conservative newspaper moves further away from that position.

</details>

**Which Macroeconomic Releases Move FOMC Prediction Markets? Evidence from Polymarket**<br>
with Basil Jan<br>
<small>*Draft available upon request*</small>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Abstract</summary>

We use 3.5 million trades on 69 Polymarket contracts for Federal Reserve rate decisions covering 16 FOMC meeting cycles (May 2024–December 2025) to measure how macroeconomic announcements shift market-implied probabilities of specific rate outcomes. Linking trade-level data to 15 macro series, we find that CPI, PCE, and NFP releases each move contract prices by approximately one percentage point within 60 minutes — roughly twice the response to FOMC rate decisions, which are partially pre-priced. About 60 percent of the 24-hour price adjustment completes within one hour. Price impacts are amplified when pre-release market uncertainty is high, consistent with Bayesian updating.

</details>

**Domestic Revenue Mobilization in Low-Income Countries**<br>
with John-Paul Fanning and Thomas Augsten<br>
<small>*Draft available upon request*</small>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Abstract</summary>

This paper investigates the effect of domestic revenue mobilization on economic growth for a sample of 65 low-income countries (LICs) over the period 1990–2019. Using local projection methods with the Arellano-Bond estimator, we analyze the impact of tax revenue increases on real GDP per capita growth and examine the transmission channels through consumption and investment. Our results suggest that a one percentage point increase in the tax-to-GDP ratio positively affects real GDP per capita growth on impact, with effects remaining statistically significant through the second year. The growth effects operate primarily through increased private consumption, while public and private investment show no significant response. These findings indicate that revenue mobilization in LICs works through consumption and confidence channels rather than investment channels, suggesting a missed opportunity to use additional fiscal resources for productivity-enhancing public investment that could support sustainable long-term growth.

</details>
